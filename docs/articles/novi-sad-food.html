The Flavours of Novi Sad: A City Told Through Its Food

6 min read
Food Worth the Trip
Home/ Serbia/ The Flavours of Novi Sad: A City Told Through Its Food
On This Trip

Fish stew, ražnjići and market stalls — a city best understood one dish at a time.

In many cities, food is simply a pleasant break between sightseeing. You explore the main square, climb to the fortress, visit a museum, and only then start thinking about lunch. Novi Sad works a little differently. Here, the local cuisine reveals as much about the city as its streets do, because almost every signature dish reflects a different chapter of its history.

River fish speaks of the Danube. Strudels and potato dumplings recall Vojvodina’s Austro-Hungarian past. The famous Index Sandwich tells the story of Novi Sad’s student culture, while Bermet wine leads beyond the city to the vineyards of Sremski Karlovci. Rather than treating local food as a checklist of dishes to try, think of it as another way to understand why Novi Sad became the city it is today.

It All Begins with the Danube

Novi Sad grew up beside one of Europe’s great rivers, and the Danube has never been just another feature of the landscape. For centuries, it served as a trade route, settlements appeared along its banks, and fishing became part of everyday life. River fish was never considered a luxury. It was simply the natural food of a city shaped by the water.

That is why menus across Novi Sad are filled with carp, catfish, pike perch, and other fish from the Danube. They are served in rich fish soup, grilled over an open fire, or cooked into fiš paprikaš, a hearty paprika-based fish stew that has become one of the region’s defining dishes. But if you really want to experience this side of Novi Sad, don’t settle for the first restaurant you find in the city centre. Instead, head to the place where river fish is part of a much bigger tradition.

A Čarda Is More Than Just a Restaurant

At first glance, a čarda may look like an ordinary riverside fish restaurant, but that would be missing the point. Traditionally, these inns stood along rivers and trade routes, welcoming fishermen, merchants, and travellers. Over time, they evolved into places where people gathered for long lunches, unhurried conversations, and slow evenings by the water.

Nobody comes to a čarda for a quick meal. People choose a table overlooking the Danube, order fish soup, fiš paprikaš, or grilled pike perch, and stay for hours. Live tamburica music often fills the air, local wine appears on the table, and the river slowly changes colour as the afternoon slips into evening.

That is why at least one meal in a čarda deserves a place in your itinerary. It reveals a side of Novi Sad that you rarely notice while walking through the historic centre. Here, the city stops being a collection of streets and squares and becomes what it has always been: a place whose rhythm has been shaped by the Danube for centuries.

Why Novi Sad’s Cuisine Doesn’t Taste Like the Serbia You Expect

“Why Novi Sad’s Cuisine Doesn’t Taste Like the Serbia You Expect”

Most visitors arrive in Serbia expecting grilled meats, ćevapi, and pljeskavica. You will certainly find them in Novi Sad, but they tell only part of the story. The city lies in Vojvodina, a region where Serbs, Hungarians, Germans, Slovaks, Rusyns, Romanians, and many other communities lived side by side for generations.

As their traditions blended, so did their food. Paprika, slow-cooked stews, homemade noodles, cured meats, poppy seeds, walnuts, and plums all found a place at the same table. Serbian meat dishes sit comfortably alongside Hungarian paprika recipes, Central European pastries, and desserts that would feel just as at home in Budapest, Bratislava, or the former towns of the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

In many ways, Novi Sad’s cuisine explains the history of Vojvodina better than any museum. The region has long stood at the crossroads of cultures, and its food preserves that shared heritage in every meal.

A Dish That Could Only Have Been Born Here

Among Novi Sad’s signature foods, the Index Sandwich feels surprisingly modern. It is a generous toasted sandwich filled with meat, cheese, and a variety of toppings. Its importance has little to do with the recipe itself and everything to do with the city where it was created.

The sandwich first appeared near the university and took its name from the index—the student record book once used throughout Yugoslavia. It was designed to be affordable, filling, and easy to eat between lectures. Over time, what began as practical student food became one of Novi Sad’s best-known culinary symbols.

Trying an Index Sandwich offers a glimpse into another side of the city. Novi Sad is not only about the fortress, elegant architecture, and the Danube. It is also one of Serbia’s largest university cities, where a vibrant student culture has helped shape its identity for decades.

Strudels, Gomboce, and a Love of Pastry

After fish dishes and hearty sandwiches, Vojvodina’s sweeter side begins to emerge. The region has a deep affection for pastry, homemade dough, poppy seeds, walnuts, and fruit fillings. These flavours arrived through Central European traditions and became so deeply woven into local life that they now feel unmistakably Vojvodinian.

One dessert you should not miss is strudel. In Vojvodina, it is commonly filled with poppy seeds, walnuts, cherries, or apples. The poppy seed version is especially rich, with a filling that often rivals the pastry itself. It bears little resemblance to the light café dessert served with a scoop of ice cream. Here, strudel feels homemade—cut into thick slices and enjoyed slowly with coffee.

Another regional favourite is gomboce. These soft potato dumplings are traditionally filled with whole plums, though other fruits are sometimes used. Once cooked, they are rolled in toasted breadcrumbs and often finished with sugar and cinnamon. Potatoes, fruit, and breadcrumbs may sound like an unusual combination, yet together they create one of Vojvodina’s most comforting desserts. Even the name reflects the Hungarian influence that still echoes through the region’s cuisine.

Bermet: A Wine Worth Leaving the City For

Novi Sad’s culinary story extends beyond the city itself. Just a short drive away lies Sremski Karlovci, a small town surrounded by vineyards and known for one of Serbia’s most distinctive wines: Bermet.

This aromatic dessert wine is infused with herbs, spices, and other botanicals, giving it a rich, complex, slightly sweet character. It is usually enjoyed in small glasses, and many producers still rely on closely guarded family recipes. Outside this part of Serbia, Bermet is surprisingly difficult to find.

Its appeal lies not in the legends that surround it, but in how naturally it completes the region’s culinary identity. The Danube gave Vojvodina its fish, the fertile plains provided grain, fruit, and poppy seeds, and the nearby hills produced the vineyards that continue to define its wine culture.

A City Best Understood Around the Table

Novi Sad’s cuisine cannot be reduced to a single idea of Serbian food. Like the city itself, it reflects centuries of cultural exchange without losing its own identity.

Fiš paprikaš tells the story of the Danube and Hungary’s influence. A meal in a čarda reveals how locals have gathered by the river for generations. Strudels and gomboce preserve Vojvodina’s Central European heritage, the Index Sandwich celebrates the city’s students, and Bermet leads you into the vineyards beyond Novi Sad.

So when you visit, don’t simply look for somewhere to eat. Let each dish become another chapter in the city’s story. You may discover that Novi Sad is understood not only by walking its streets, but also by sitting at its tables, where the Danube, Vojvodina, and centuries of shared history come together on a single menu.
